## 2. Executive Summary

**The problem.** Manchester has a documented student-HMO waste crisis. 4–8 students share one wheelie bin per HMO, contamination and overspill are routine, and the fines for landlords are escalating fast. Manchester City Council issued more fly-tipping fines than any other authority in England in 2023/24 (£130,034 total, up 32% year-on-year),¹ has adopted a nine-year war-on-rubbish strategy through 2035,² and warns landlords explicitly that they face up to £30,000 civil penalties per HMO licence breach and up to £50,000 fines under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 for end-of-tenancy waste failures.³ Manchester Student Homes published "Fly-tipping is rubbish" in February 2025 codifying the landlord's legal exposure.⁴ Nationally, fly-tipping in England hit 1.26 million recorded incidents in 2024/25, a 9% increase on the prior year and the highest figure ever recorded.⁵

**The solution.** BinRoute is a weekly bin concierge contracted by the landlord, not the tenant. For £15 per house per week, a small uniformed team visits every contracted HMO on the day before collection, sorts contamination, puts the right bins out the right way, retrieves them after collection, and photo-documents every visit so the landlord has audit-trail evidence that they have discharged their duty of care. The model converts a £50,000 catastrophic-risk tail into a £780/year predictable line item.

**The market.** Manchester has ~13,500 licensed HMOs in Greater Manchester⁶ and an Article 4 student-HMO belt centred on Fallowfield, Withington, Rusholme, Hulme and Ardwick.⁷ A serviceable addressable market of roughly 3,000 student HMOs in Fallowfield/Withington/Rusholme alone yields £2.3m of recurring revenue at full penetration in one corridor of one city. The combined UK opportunity across the top 10 student cities is approximately £30m+ in addressable annual recurring revenue.

**The strategic asset.** The founder will activate a student-facing reporting campaign in Fallowfield/Withington/Rusholme, encouraging students to report fly-tipping incidents to Manchester City Council using its existing reporting tool.⁸ This is not a marketing gimmick — it is a calibrated demand-creation lever. More reports → more council enforcement attention → more landlords with active fines or near-misses → a pre-warmed sales market. The campaign also produces aggregable data for press placements in *The Mancunion*, *The Tab Manchester* and *Manchester Evening News*, and embeds BinRoute in the community narrative around environmental degradation. The model is replicable verbatim in every new city BinRoute enters.

**The economics.** A single route of 25 houses generates £375/week revenue at £15/house. With a one-person experienced operator paid £15/hr for ~5 hours' actual route time, gross weekly margin is approximately £300 per route before overheads. The model breaks even at roughly 20 contracted houses. At 100 houses, BinRoute is generating £78,000 annual revenue with a ~55% gross margin. At national scale (5,000 houses across 10 cities), the business runs at £3.9m revenue and credible 25–30% net margins.

**The traction plan.** Sign the first 20 houses in 4 weeks via a single Manchester Student Homes-listed letting agent partnership. Get to 100 houses by month 6. Get to a second city (Leeds — Headingley) by month 12. By month 24, run 500 houses across 4 cities.

**The ask.** This brief models three funding scenarios. Bootstrap is viable. A £20,000 angel cheque accelerates the Manchester ramp from 18 to 9 months. A £50,000 raise unlocks City 2 in month 9 and City 3 in month 18. A £250,000 institutional cheque buys five cities in two years.

**National scalability in one sentence.** The Manchester launch is proof of concept for a UK-wide rollout into every Article 4 student-HMO city — the regulatory pressure is national, the operational template is identical, and the reporting-campaign playbook is portable to every council that publishes a fly-tipping reporting tool.

## 3. The Problem

### 3.1 What the regulator does

Manchester City Council operates one of the most aggressive municipal enforcement regimes in England against household and commercial waste offences.

- In 2023/24, Manchester issued **£130,034 in fly-tipping fines — the highest of any English authority** — a 32% rise on 2022/23.¹
- The council conducted 5,794 investigations into fly-tipping, leading to 1,070 fines and 334 prosecutions in 2023/24.¹
- In February 2026 the council approved a nine-year **Keep Manchester Tidy** strategy through 2035, explicitly committing to expanded CCTV, harder enforcement, and behaviour-change campaigns.²
- The council operates an **Environmental Crimes Team** that prosecutes cases at Manchester Magistrates' Court. Recent 2024 prosecutions include a driving-banned offender for an alleyway fly-tip behind student-occupied homes.⁹
- WISE Enforcement Officers are contracted to issue Fixed Penalty Notices on behalf of the council, with FPN authority across the city.¹⁰
- Manchester recorded **14,963 fly-tipping incidents in 2024/25** — comparable in volume to small London boroughs.¹⁵
- Recent council social-media posts confirm that residents' tip-offs underpin a substantial share of these prosecutions: a single week in mid-2025 produced four offenders fined a combined £24,694 plus one 18-week suspended prison sentence, with the council explicitly thanking "tip-offs from residents."¹¹

### 3.2 Why this hits HMO landlords harder than anyone else

Manchester Student Homes — the joint UoM/MMU/Salford/RNCM accreditation scheme — published "Fly-tipping is rubbish" in February 2025 setting out the landlord legal position in unambiguous terms:⁴

> *"Most student rental properties containing at least three tenants are classed as Houses in Multiple Occupation (HMOs)... Landlords of licensable HMOs can face civil penalties of up to £30,000 per breach if they fail to meet licence conditions, including not providing enough bins or failing to give tenants guidance on waste disposal. Under the Environmental Protection Act 1990, landlords who fail to deal with end-of-tenancy waste properly can face fines of up to £50,000 and potentially five years imprisonment."*

This is not theoretical. The legal architecture combines three statutes:
- **Environmental Protection Act 1990, s.34** — duty of care on the producer of waste.
- **EPA 1990, s.46/46A** — power for collection authorities to serve receptacle-use notices on occupiers and on persons "by reason of whose action" non-compliance occurred, with summary conviction fines following.¹²
- **HMO licensing conditions under the Housing Act 2004** — Manchester licence conditions require "adequate waste storage facilities" and tenant guidance, with up to £30,000 civil penalties per breach.¹³

The structural problem: a 6-person student HMO produces 4–5× the waste of a typical family home but is allocated a single 240-litre wheelie bin on a fortnightly collection.¹⁴ The bin overflows, tenants leave bags in alleyways, the alleyway becomes magnetic for additional fly-tipping from third parties, the council issues an enforcement notice — and the legal route runs back to the licensable landlord.

### 3.3 The national context — this is not a Manchester quirk

England recorded **1.26 million fly-tipping incidents in 2024/25**, up 9% year-on-year and the highest figure in the series.⁵ Underlying components:

| Category | 2024/25 | YoY change |
|---|---|---|
| Total incidents | 1.26m | +9% |
| Roads & highways incidents | 463,000 | +9% |
| Footpath/bridgeway incidents | 254,000 | +17% |
| Council-land incidents (estates, parks, car parks) | 223,000 | +13% |
| Large-scale ("tipper lorry or larger") incidents | 52,000 | +11% |
| Enforcement actions | 572,000 | +8% |
| Fixed Penalty Notices issued | 69,000 | +9% |
| Court fines (count) | 1,250 | -9% |
| Court fines (value) | £673,000 | -8% |

The macro picture is unambiguous: incidents are rising, FPNs are rising, but court prosecutions are falling and being substituted with administrative civil penalties — which are the exact instrument that hits HMO landlords. The cost to councils of clearing the 52,000 large-scale incidents alone reached £19.3m in 2024/25.⁵

### 3.4 Why Manchester first

The combination that creates the Manchester opportunity:

1. **Highest enforcement-fines-issued of any English authority in the most recent published year** (2023/24).¹
2. **One of the densest student-HMO concentrations in the UK** — Fallowfield is approximately 54% student;¹⁶ Manchester's PBSA shortfall pushes ~50,000+ students into the southern HMO belt.¹⁶
3. **An active Article 4 framework** that has effectively frozen the supply of new HMOs in the student belt — meaning incumbent landlords cannot dilute the problem by adding more bin-permitted properties.⁷
4. **A council that publicly attributes prosecutions to resident reporting** — which is the demand-creation lever BinRoute leverages.¹¹
5. **A founder embedded inside the student population**, with University of Manchester access, native social-media reach, and a credible finance-professional sales register for landlord conversations.

### 3.5 The student-side narrative — already building, no longer dormant

Manchester Student Homes published its February 2025 "Fly-tipping is rubbish" piece;⁴ S4B Manchester ran a "Saying NO to Fly Tipping" community campaign in June 2025;¹⁷ Recycle for Greater Manchester runs an active anti-fly-tipping landing page.¹⁸ The community narrative is in motion. BinRoute does not need to start a movement — it needs to amplify and instrumentalise one that already exists.

## 4. The Solution

### 4.1 What BinRoute physically does

Every contracted HMO receives one weekly visit on the day before the council collection day. The standard visit:

1. **Pre-sort and decontaminate.** The operator opens each bin and removes obvious contamination (plastic films from the brown bin, food waste from the blue bin, etc., per Recycle for Greater Manchester rules¹⁹). Contamination is the leading cause of bin-rejection notices that cascade into overflow and fly-tipping.
2. **Move to kerb.** Bins are wheeled from rear yards and side passages to the kerbside, oriented correctly. In Article 4 alleyways, this is the single highest-friction task that tenants neglect.
3. **Photo-document.** Each bin is photographed with timestamp and GPS metadata. The photo set is uploaded to the landlord portal as proof of service.
4. **Post-collection retrieve.** After the lorry has passed (typically a 4–8 hour window), the operator returns to bring the bins back inside the property line. This is the single most common council enforcement trigger: bins left on the pavement for more than 24 hours can attract a Section 46 notice.¹²
5. **Excess-waste flagging.** If there is more waste than fits the contracted bin capacity (e.g. end-of-tenancy build-up), the operator either bags and removes it as a chargeable add-on or escalates to the landlord through the portal.

### 4.2 What the landlord is actually buying

The landlord is not buying labour. The landlord is buying:
- **Documentary evidence** that they have provided adequate waste-management arrangements (an HMO licence condition).
- **A first line of defence** against the §46 EPA notice → FPN → civil penalty escalation pathway.
- **Insurance against the worst-case** £50,000 EPA 1990 prosecution.
- **A repair item** that does not get reported on Fixflo at 11pm on a Tuesday.
- **A retention tool** at re-let time, because every Manchester student knows the bin problem and a properly managed house lets faster.

### 4.3 Why £15/week is the right price

The £15/week price point sits on three pieces of triangulation:

| Anchor | Number | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Catastrophic fine ceiling | £50,000 | EPA 1990 max — the landlord's tail risk |
| HMO licence civil penalty | up to £30,000/breach | Per Manchester licence breach |
| Typical FPN | £400 (Manchester); rising to £1,000 in London (Croydon)²⁰ | Routine enforcement cost |
| Annual BinRoute cost | £780/house (£15 × 52) | The line item |
| Annual cost as % of rent | ~2.5% on a £30,000-rent HMO | Trivial relative to repairs, utilities, council tax disputes |
| Comparable spend categories | Gardening £400–800/year, gas safety £80, EICR £200/5y | BinRoute sits comfortably inside the landlord's existing recurring-cost mental model |

A behavioural-economics anchor: even a 1.6% subjective probability of a £50,000 fine in any given year makes BinRoute an expected-value win for the landlord (£780 premium vs £800 expected loss). Manchester's current fine issuance — 1,070 fines across roughly 13,500 GM HMOs in 2023/24 — implies an empirical landlord-level fine-per-year probability already approaching ~8% even before the council's nine-year escalation. The price is right; the question is sales.

The price does need to vary by city. London — where Croydon's FPN floor has already moved to £1,000²⁰ and waste sector wages are 15–20% higher — should be priced at £20–25/week per house. The pricing matrix is set out in §11.

### 4.4 Why a landlord (not a tenant) contract

This is the single most important architectural decision in the model.

| Dimension | Landlord-paid | Tenant-paid |
|---|---|---|
| Customer life | 5–15 years (property hold period) | 9 months |
| Decision unit | 1 person (the owner) | 4–8 people (a fragmented house) |
| Willingness to pay | Anchored to a £50k fine | Anchored to £3.75/month per head |
| Renewal friction | Auto-renews with management contract | Re-sells every July |
| Default risk | Low (a property cashflow line) | High (student arrears) |
| Sales cost per £1 of LTV | Low | Very high |

The brief explicitly disregards the tenant-as-customer pivot. The student-facing thesis is operationally hostile and economically nine times less valuable per signed customer. Students show up only on the demand-creation side, never the revenue side.

### 5.4 Demand creation through the reporting campaign

This is where the addressable market is grown, not just harvested.

Manchester City Council operates an online reporting tool accessed at manchester.gov.uk/flytipping plus phone (0300 555 0128), WhatsApp, and online contact form.⁸ The council confirms that **resident tip-offs underpin a substantial share of prosecutions**¹¹ and the Environmental Crimes Team explicitly invites public reporting.⁹

The campaign mechanic, in plain English: take the 116,000-strong Manchester student population and convert a measurable share of it into active reporters. Every additional report increases the visible pressure on the council to issue enforcement notices to specific addresses. Enforcement notices on specific addresses produce nervous landlords. Nervous landlords are pre-qualified BinRoute prospects.

Quantitative working assumption — the campaign converts 1–3% of the Manchester student population into one-time reporters in its first 6 months. At 116,000 students that is 1,160–3,480 incremental reports. Against a 2024/25 baseline of 14,963 incidents in Manchester,¹⁵ that is a credible 8–23% uplift in reporting volume — sufficient to be visible to the council, sufficient to drive press coverage, but not large enough to flood the system or to be dismissed as orchestrated noise.

This is treated as a strategic asset throughout the brief (sections 7, 10, 11, 13, 14).

### 5.5 Manchester market growth trajectory

The student HMO market is structurally growing, not contracting:
- UoM student numbers grew from ~40,000 to ~46,000 between 2018 and 2024.
- Manchester's PBSA pipeline (12,500 beds by 2030) does not close the bed gap; the shortfall is structural.
- The Article 4 directions prevent new HMO supply, increasing demand pressure on the existing 3,500 properties.
- Per the Renters' Rights Act 2025, ASTs are being replaced by periodic tenancies with mandatory written terms — increasing landlord administrative load and creating demand for "compliance-as-a-service" categories that BinRoute occupies.

The combined effect: the student-HMO market is fixed-supply, demand-led, and increasingly compliance-loaded. This is favourable for any compliance-adjacent service.

## 7. Business Model

### 7.1 Revenue model

| Component | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Headline product** | Weekly bin concierge — £15/house/week (Manchester base price) |
| **Contract structure** | 12-month rolling, billed monthly in advance via Direct Debit (GoCardless) |

### 7.2 Pricing rationale

The £15/week point survives three triangulation tests:

1. **Willingness-to-pay calculation** — a 1.6% subjective probability of a £50k fine in any year creates an expected loss of £800. £780/year for BinRoute is below that breakeven. The empirical fine-frequency for Manchester (1,070 fines / ~13,500 HMOs in 2023/24) implies an actual landlord-level annual probability already approaching 8% — making BinRoute a 10:1 expected-value win.

2. **Comparable services anchor** — gardening £400–800/year, gas safety £80, EICR £200/5y, end-of-tenancy clean £150–300, six-monthly inspection by an agent £100–200. £780/year sits inside the existing landlord cost vocabulary.

3. **Route economics floor** — at £15/house × 25 houses, route revenue is £375/week; below £12/house the route stops being profitable for a fully-loaded experienced worker (see §8).

The price has elasticity. A 6-month landlord pilot in months 4–9 should test £12/£15/£18 in parallel cohorts to confirm.

## 8. Workforce Strategy

### 8.1 The structural context — real wages have collapsed for refuse workers

This is the single most important macro fact for the workforce strategy. The case for hiring experienced bin workers is not "pay them slightly more than minimum wage" — it is "the market has structurally undervalued these workers for a decade, and a small operator can offer them a genuinely better deal."

Key inputs (verified via current research):

- Between December 2019 and November 2023, **inflation-adjusted public sector pay fell 0.3%** while private sector pay grew 2.3%.
- Real public sector pay at end-2023 was **1% lower than 2007** — a 16-year freeze.³⁴
- The sharpest fall occurred early 2022–spring 2023: an average **£2,000 (6%) drop in real terms**, double the private-sector decline.³⁴
- GMB has formally rejected the 2025/26 LGA pay offer of 3.2% as inadequate; the union characterises refuse workers as systematically undervalued and "realising their worth" as adjacent roles pay more.³⁵
- The annual regular pay growth in real terms (CPIH-adjusted) was just 0.5% in Aug–Oct 2025.³⁴
- Veolia refuse loader average wage in the UK: ~£10/hour, £21,309/year, 90th-percentile £29,712.³¹
- Biffa Manchester: pay distribution skewed to ~£10–12/hour for frontline.³¹
- Manchester City Council waste operative pay sits on Greater Manchester local-authority pay scales (SCP 3–8 range), generally £24,000–£26,400/year — equivalent to roughly £12.30–£13.50/hour.

**Strategic conclusion.** A council refuse worker earning ~£12.80/hour, who has experienced six years of below-inflation pay settlements and is in a union actively recommending strike action, is structurally receptive to a part-time supplementary income paying £15/hour for 5–10 hours per week on top of their day job — or to a full switch at a premium hourly rate. The £15/hour BinRoute offer is **17% above council median** and **50% above Veolia refuse loader average**. This is not exploitative; it is the wage these workers should have been earning a decade ago. The pitch is honest.

### 8.3 Recommendation — start hybrid, lean experienced as you scale

**Launch model (months 1–6): Hybrid.**
- 1 experienced lead worker (the founder personally for the first 2 months; then a recruited ex-council operator from month 3) per route.
- 1 paid student helper per route from month 4 onwards for cost dilution and recruitment of additional helpers as routes proliferate.
- Founder runs the first route personally to build operational knowledge and to model the work he is asking employees to do.

**Mature model (months 6+): Experienced-led with student helpers.**
- 1 experienced lead per 2 routes (50 houses), responsible for route quality and contamination escalations.
- 2–3 student helpers per route for the physical work.
- This converges on the hybrid economics in §8.2 (61% margin, 5 reliability, 4 quality).

The reason not to launch student-only despite its higher headline margin is the **single-point-of-failure problem** (§8.5) and the **reliability variance** that compounds at small scale. A 25-house route with a no-show on bin day causes 25 simultaneous failures — and on a first contract with a new letting agent, the contract dies.

### 8.4 Resolving the poaching-vs-council-relations tension

This is the sharpest contradiction in the model and demands a specific, named approach rather than a hedge.

**The tension.** BinRoute's demand-creation strategy depends on the council enforcing more aggressively. The council enforces through refuse-worker observation, environmental crime team reporting, and FPN issuance. Actively recruiting from the council workforce risks souring the relationship with the institution whose enforcement underpins the entire business model.

**The recommended approach: a clean separation between "the institution" and "the individual."**

1. **Never approach a council employee at work or about their council role.** All recruitment happens through public job ads, GMB-affiliated channels (where appropriate), and referrals. BinRoute job ads simply state "experience preferred; refuse, recycling, facilities maintenance background welcomed" and let applicants self-select.

2. **Target ex-council workers and commercial waste workers, not active council employees.** The pool of recently-departed council refuse workers (resignations, retirements, role changes) is sufficient to staff Year 1 without poaching from active rotas. Commercial waste sector workers at Biffa, Veolia, Suez and FCC also have transferable skills and weaker emotional ties to the council enforcement narrative.³¹

3. **Frame the offer publicly as wage justice, not as competition.** BinRoute's external positioning — including media placements alongside the reporting campaign — should explicitly cite the GMB / refuse worker real-wage data and position the business as paying refuse workers what they are worth. This makes a council attempt at retaliation look bad for the council.³⁵

4. **Don't court PR conflict with the council.** Avoid the "us vs them" framing. The reporting campaign is positioned as a community service to the council ("we're helping you do your job better"), not as an adversarial pressure tactic. The council's stated 2026 strategy explicitly relies on resident reporting.²

5. **If a serving council worker applies anyway, route them through a 30-day notice "transition" period.** Don't induce a sudden departure that would attract attention.

**Reputational risk net assessment.** With this approach, the residual risk is low (Likelihood 2 / Impact 3 in the risk register, §14). The council's institutional incentive is to want fly-tipping reduced; if BinRoute demonstrably reduces incident volumes in its serviced corridors, the council benefits.

### 8.5 Workforce risk — single point of failure

**The contingency plan for a sick-day on a live route:**

| Layer | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| 1 — Cross-cover | From month 4, no route is staffed by a single person — at minimum a 60/40 split between lead and helper |
| 2 — Buffer pool | A standing pool of 2–3 "on-call" student workers paid a £10 retainer to be available on collection mornings |
| 3 — Founder backstop | For the first 12 months, the founder personally covers any uncovered route as a non-delegable obligation |
| 4 — Scheduling software | Deputy or Connecteam (~£15/operator/month) for shift management once headcount >5 |
| 5 — SLA construction | The landlord contract specifies "service in the day of, or before next-scheduled collection cycle" — not a fixed time slot — preserving operational slack |

### 8.6 Seasonal vulnerability — students leave in summer

Manchester student bin collections continue year-round; landlords still pay year-round; students largely vacate Fallowfield from late June to mid-September. Three responses:

1. **Year-round contract** (the default) — landlord pays £15/wk for 52 weeks even though tenant occupancy is 38–45 weeks. The summer service is just as valuable because (a) end-of-tenancy waste is the peak fly-tipping pressure point, (b) bins still need moving on collection day even if empty, (c) the landlord retains the audit trail.
2. **Summer-intensive add-on** (premium tier) — bundled end-of-tenancy clearance in late June / early July monetises the highest-pain landlord moment of the year. This is the obvious natural upsell.
3. **Worker availability** — the experienced (non-student) lead worker is the year-round constant. Student helpers can be supplemented in summer with international students staying in Manchester (Salford has one of the UK's largest year-round international cohorts).

### 10.3 The reporting campaign in detail

**Brand & positioning.**
- Brand: "BinHero" — a separate but adjacent identity to KerbCrew/BinRoute.
- Positioning: community-led, student-fronted, environmentally-progressive. Tagline: "If it's wrong, report it. If it's right, share it."
- Why a separate brand: it preserves the option for KerbCrew to remain a B2B service brand; BinHero becomes the community-facing front. If the campaign provokes any reputational complications, BinRoute can ringfence them.

**Mechanics, week by week.**

| Phase | Activity | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-launch (weeks 1–2) | Build Instagram + TikTok + linktree. Reach out to UoM Climate Network, MMU Sustainability Society, Salford Eco Society, Manchester FoE for endorsements. | £0 |
| Soft launch (weeks 3–6) | First 20 organic content pieces: "How to report a fly-tip in Fallowfield" (3-step explainer); "What happens when you report" (council process walkthrough); "Worst alleyway of the week" community gallery (NEVER showing identifiable people/plates). | £0 |
| Acceleration (weeks 7–16) | Weekly community report — aggregated MCC report numbers (publicly available via FixMyStreet³⁶ and council transparency releases). Run a "Tidy Street" leaderboard. Submit FOI requests for fines issued by postcode and publish. | £0–£50 (FOI is free; postage for any physical version) |
| Press pitch (week 12) | Aggregated data + a sympathetic Fallowfield local councillor + a quote from MSH = a *Manchester Evening News* "students fight back against fly-tipping" piece. | £0 |
| Sustained (months 4+) | Monthly community report + university press placements; expand to TikTok. Founder appears as the "founder of a Manchester student venture trying to fix it." | £0 |

**Call to action.** Single linktree URL with one obvious button: "Report a fly-tip in 2 minutes" → deep links to the MCC reporting form pre-populated with postcode. Subordinate buttons: "Tag a tip we should photograph" / "Find your bin day" / "Join the team."

**Ethical guardrails.**
- The campaign never identifies individuals or property occupants.
- The campaign never coaches users to file false reports — only to report what they observe.
- The campaign never paid for or solicited reports in exchange for value.
- The campaign explicitly publishes its methodology and links to the MCC's own reporting page; it is a megaphone for the council's existing system, not a parallel system.
- If the council ever asks BinHero to wind down or change its messaging, BinHero complies in the same news cycle. Compliance with the council is more valuable than the campaign itself.

**Why this is genuinely different from "orchestrated reporting."** Every report is submitted by a real person about a real incident they have observed. The campaign provides information, not incentives. This is the same mechanism by which Surfers Against Sewage's *Safer Seas* tool transformed the UK water-quality debate³⁷ — citizens reporting real conditions, aggregated by a campaigning organisation, then taken seriously by regulators. The Surfers Against Sewage precedent demonstrates that civic-reporting infrastructures, even when run by a non-impartial organisation, achieve regulatory traction when they are evidence-led.

### 11.1 The standardised city-entry playbook

Each new city follows an identical four-phase template, total entry time approximately 12 weeks.

**Phase 0 — Pre-launch reconnaissance (weeks -8 to -4)**
- Pull the city's published fly-tipping data from DEFRA / council transparency portal.
- Map the student HMO district(s) and identify the equivalent of Fallowfield (Headingley in Leeds, Selly Oak in Birmingham, Lenton in Nottingham, Broomhill in Sheffield, Jesmond in Newcastle).
- Build a letting-agent shortlist (top 20 by HMO portfolio size in the target district).
- Identify the local student press, the local SU sustainability society, and the local environmental NGO.
- Submit FOI to the council requesting fly-tipping incidents and enforcement actions in the target postcodes (free, ~20 working day response).

**Phase 1 — Reporting campaign activation (weeks -4 to 0)**
- Spin up a city-prefixed Instagram/TikTok handle (e.g. BinHeroLeeds).
- Adapt Manchester content: same templates, swap postcode references and council reporting URLs.
- Endorsement collection: 3–5 student society partnerships before launch.
- Pre-place a press story with the local student paper.

**Phase 2 — Soft launch (weeks 0–4)**
- Founder or operations manager spends 3 weeks in-city.
- Recruit 1 experienced lead worker (Indeed + GMB local + local Unitemps).
- Run founder-led first 1–2 routes for 4 weeks.
- Sign 2–3 letting-agent contracts.

**Phase 3 — Scale (weeks 4–12)**
- Hand over to local operations lead.
- Target 100 houses by end of Phase 3.
- City achieves cashflow positivity by week 16–20.

### 11.2 City-entry milestone triggers

The trigger to open City 2 is not a vague "when Manchester is going well" — it is specific.

| Trigger | Threshold |

| Manchester contracted houses | ≥150 |

### 11.5 Is the reporting campaign equally effective everywhere?

No, and this should be acknowledged.

| City profile | Campaign role |
|---|---|
| Manchester, Leeds, Nottingham, Sheffield, Newcastle, Bristol | **Full demand-creation mode** — enforcement is rising but not at saturation; campaign meaningfully expands the addressable market |
| Croydon, Camden, Camberwell (London) | **Pure brand-building** — enforcement is already at £1,000/FPN saturation level; campaign serves brand/community function, not market warming |
| Smaller cities (Lancaster, Durham, Bath) | **Lower priority** — student population thinner; campaign less impactful given the scale of available demand uplift |

**Implication for sequencing:** the campaign-leveraged cities should be entered first. London goes last, where the demand exists but the campaign mechanism is no longer needed and the operational cost is highest.

### 11.6 The operational risks of expanding too fast

The single biggest failure mode is **service quality degradation in the home market caused by founder attention diffusion in a new market**. Specific guards:

1. The 6-trigger threshold in §11.2.
2. A "Manchester first, always" service prioritisation rule — if Manchester operations are stressed, the founder returns and pauses City 2 launch activity.
3. A 90-day operational pause between Cities 2 and 3 (no two new launches within a single quarter).

The single biggest failure mode of expanding too slowly is **competitive moat erosion** if a national service-business roll-up notices the category. The risk register (§14) addresses this explicitly.

## 13. Competitive Landscape

### 13.1 Direct competitors — currently zero

There is no UK service that combines (a) weekly cadence, (b) landlord billing, (c) HMO-specific positioning and (d) photo-audit-trail delivery in the £10–20/week price band. The closest adjacencies all fail at least one of those tests:

| Operator | What they do | Why not a direct threat |
|---|---|---|
| **Clearabee** | One-off bulky waste removal, skip bags, manned clearance.²⁹ Private home wheelie bin collection product exists but is positioned as ad-hoc, not weekly subscription | Wrong cadence, wrong customer (tenant/homeowner) |
| **Junk Hunters** | London-focused waste clearance; per-volume pricing | Wrong city, wrong cadence |
| **The Rubbish Removers (Manchester)** | Local clearance, listed on MCC's HSM directory | Wrong cadence, no compliance positioning |
| **Biffa / Veolia / Suez** | Industrial waste contractors. Council contracts. Commercial sector | No HMO landlord SKU; HMO is too small to be interesting on existing pricing |
| **Council garden waste subscription** | £40/year for a green-waste bin | Not the same problem; doesn't touch residual or recycling chaos |

### 13.2 The five most dangerous potential competitors

**1 — A national property management platform adds bin-management as a feature.**
The single biggest existential threat. Fixflo + Arthur Online already integrate to manage HMO compliance³⁸ — bins would be a logical extension. Goodlord could add it; UnPaperApp / Reposit could add it. Their advantage: existing landlord billing relationships, automated invoicing. Their disadvantage: they don't have feet on the ground; they would have to subcontract physical delivery. Mitigation: BinRoute should become the subcontracted physical delivery layer for one of these platforms before they build it themselves. A platform partnership in months 18–24 is a defensive priority.

**2 — A regional waste company spins up a residential subscription division.**
A medium-risk threat. Biffa or Suez UK could decide a residential subscription is worth £5m of annual revenue in a target city. Their advantage: vehicles, drivers, depots, brand. Their disadvantage: the operational structure (HGV-led, depot-routed, council-contract-anchored) is poorly suited to the high-touch low-density HMO concierge SKU. Mitigation: BinRoute's moat is route density built early; if Biffa enters Manchester after BinRoute has 800 houses, the marginal recruitment cost for Biffa is high.

**3 — A national domestic services roll-up acquires multiple regional bin operators.**
A medium-risk threat. The classic UK roll-up template (PestSmart, Helping Hands, Acorn Recycling). Mitigation: be the acquirable consolidator, not the acquiree — see §15.

**4 — A new entrant copies the model with venture capital backing.**
A medium-risk threat. The business has no patentable IP. A well-funded copy could outspend BinRoute on Year 1 sales hires. Mitigation: route density and community-brand authenticity (the BinHero campaign) are the real moats; capital alone doesn't reproduce them.

**5 — A council insources a similar service.**
Lowest-risk. Council financial constraints make this unlikely; if it happens, it likely captures only mandatory-licensed HMOs and creates a parallel market for everything else.

### 13.3 The moat

| Moat layer | Source | Defensibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Route density** | First-mover signing of clustered properties in Fallowfield → Withington → Rusholme | High — a competitor entering Manchester after BinRoute has 500 houses faces a 60–70% cost disadvantage on the first 50 houses they sign |
| **Community brand (BinHero)** | The reporting campaign embeds BinRoute in the local environmental narrative | High — cannot be replicated with money; takes 12–18 months to build |
| **Letting agent relationships** | Manchester student housing market is concentrated in ~30 organisations; sign 8 of them and the next entrant has to fight for what's left | Medium — relationship-based, not contractual |
| **Process IP** | Photo-audit portal, landlord reporting, contamination identification | Low — easily reproduced |
| **Founder credibility** | UoM affiliation, finance professional pedigree, local press relationships | Medium — non-transferable on exit but supportive during build |
| **National playbook** | A repeatable city-entry template — by City 3 the playbook is documented and reproducible | High at scale; weak at outset |

The two moats that matter most are **route density** and the **community brand built through BinHero**. Capital alone does not replicate either. A competitor with £2m of seed funding entering Manchester in Month 12 would still take 18 months and £500k+ to catch up — by which time BinRoute is in Leeds.

### 13.4 The property-management platform threat — assessed honestly

Of the five threats, the platform threat (Fixflo / Arthur / Goodlord) is the only one that could obsolete the entire category by adding a feature. Honest assessment:

- **They are unlikely to want to deliver physical service.** Software platforms do not run vans. They will outsource physical delivery to someone.
- **The first physical operator they outsource to in each city becomes the de facto national operator.** BinRoute's strategic objective by Month 18 should be to make itself that operator.
- **The platform partnership has a price.** Selling at 20–30% margin to Fixflo halves the gross margin. But it eliminates customer acquisition cost (CAC) — and CAC is the single largest hidden cost in the model.
- **Defensive pre-emption is the right answer.** By Month 12, BinRoute should have had at least one conversation with Fixflo about a Manchester pilot. Even if no deal results, the conversation deters in-house build.

The founder is a penultimate-year BSc Economics student at the University of Manchester, with completed internships at Aon and Mercer (investment consulting) and an upcoming summer placement at M&G Investments (private markets). This is his first operational venture.

He is 20 years old, lives in Fallowfield, has read the council's nine-year fly-tipping strategy, has built the financial model himself, and has the social-media native fluency to run the BinHero campaign in a way a 45-year-old former Biffa manager could not. ### E. CIC / social enterprise option

Manchester's BinRoute / KerbCrew commercial entity could in principle incorporate as a Community Interest Company (CIC) limited by shares. Key trade-offs:

| Dimension | Standard Ltd | CIC (limited by shares) |
|---|---|---|
| Access to grants | Limited (commercial only) | Wider — Power to Change, council waste-prevention grants, etc.⁴¹ |
| Profit distribution | Unrestricted | Capped at 35% asset-lock dividend |
| Investor return profile | Standard | Capped — unattractive to angel/seed funds |
| Brand authority for the campaign side | Standard | Stronger — CIC status validates community positioning |
| Regulatory overhead | Companies House | Companies House + CIC Regulator (annual community-interest report) |

**Recommendation.** Incorporate the **commercial trading entity (KerbCrew Ltd)** as a standard limited company to preserve investor flexibility. Operate **BinHero** as either (a) an unincorporated community campaign branded under KerbCrew, or (b) a standalone CIC limited by guarantee, owned operationally by the founder but with a community board. The CIC route for BinHero specifically opens grant funding from Power to Change (which distributes £20m+ p.a. to community businesses⁴¹), West Sussex-style waste-prevention grants, and council partnership funding — at the cost of additional governance.

The cleanest structure: KerbCrew Ltd (the for-profit trading entity) sponsors BinHero CIC (the community campaign), with a small annual contribution to BinHero's operating costs. This separation also derisks the poaching-vs-council tension (§8.4) by giving each side of the operation a discrete corporate identity.
